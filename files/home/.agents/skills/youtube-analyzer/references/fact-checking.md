# Fact-checking workflow

Use this workflow when the user explicitly asks to verify, investigate, validate, or
debunk claims in a video. The transcript establishes what was said; it does not
establish that the statement is true.

## Establish scope

Record the video's title, channel, upload date, caption language, and relevant
speaker identity. State whether the check evaluates claims as they would reasonably
have been understood when the video was published, against current knowledge, or
both. Default to both when later developments materially change a verdict.

Inventory the video's externally checkable claims before researching. Prioritize:

1. Claims central to the video's conclusion.
2. Claims with meaningful health, safety, financial, legal, civic, or reputational
   consequences.
3. Claims that are surprising, quantitative, causal, or attributed to named research.
4. Representative claims needed to assess a repeated argument.

Do not disguise an opinion, prediction, moral judgment, or unfalsifiable statement as
a factual claim. Identify it separately when it matters to the argument. For a long
video, state what was sampled or omitted and why; do not imply exhaustive coverage.

## Research each material claim

1. Copy or faithfully paraphrase the claim and attach its actual transcript timestamp.
2. Search for the original evidence rather than for pages agreeing with the claim.
3. Prefer primary sources: original papers and datasets, statutes and court opinions,
   official statistics and records, standards, first-party technical documentation,
   and direct statements from the responsible organization.
4. Use high-quality secondary sources to find or interpret primary material, especially
   when specialist context is required, but do not let a search snippet carry a
   verdict.
5. Open and read the supporting source. Record its title, publisher, publication date,
   and direct URL. Check whether it actually supports the precise scope, population,
   timeframe, quantity, and causal strength of the video claim.
6. Seek disconfirming evidence for consequential or disputed claims. Explain material
   disagreement among credible sources.
7. Check dates. Do not use evidence published later as though the speaker could have
   known it at upload time; instead distinguish “supported at publication” from
   “outdated or contradicted by later evidence.”

Do not silently strengthen or weaken a speaker's wording. Watch for omitted baselines,
relative-versus-absolute risk, correlation presented as causation, cherry-picked time
ranges, nonrepresentative examples, and claims generalized beyond a study population.

## Assign verdicts conservatively

Use only these verdicts:

- **Supported** — reliable evidence supports the claim as stated, including its scope.
- **Mostly supported** — the core is supported, with a minor qualification that does
  not reverse the takeaway.
- **Misleading / missing context** — technically grounded or partly true, but wording,
  omissions, scope, or presentation creates a materially wrong impression.
- **Unsupported** — the cited or discoverable evidence does not establish the claim.
- **Contradicted** — reliable evidence directly conflicts with the claim.
- **Unverifiable** — available evidence is insufficient, inaccessible, or the claim is
  too vague to test. This is not the same as false.

Match confidence to evidence quality. Say what would be needed to resolve an
`Unverifiable` verdict. Never infer a factual verdict from captions, slides, view
counts, confidence of delivery, or lack of an immediately found source.

## Deliver the report

Start with a two- or three-sentence bottom line that reflects the whole checked set,
not only the most dramatic error. Then provide a ledger:

`Timestamp | Claim | Verdict | Evidence and reasoning`

For every row:

- Link the timestamp to the source video.
- Attribute the claim to the speaker.
- Link directly to the strongest supporting and contradicting sources.
- State the reasoning needed to connect evidence to verdict.
- Include source publication dates when timing matters.

Close with:

- **What holds up** — the strongest supported parts of the argument.
- **What does not** — errors, exaggerations, or missing context and their consequence.
- **Limits** — caption uncertainty, inaccessible sources, sampling choices, and
  unresolved claims.
- **Sources** — a compact list of the primary sources actually used.

Flag likely caption errors and distinguish slide text from spoken words. Use short
quotations only when exact wording matters. Report a material disagreement plainly;
do not rewrite the video into a more defensible argument than the speaker made.
